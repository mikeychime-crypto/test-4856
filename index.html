Chapter One

The Girl Who Bent the Dark

as remembered by Lyra

Ashenmoor sat where the good light gave out — the last town before the land itself seemed to lose its nerve and go pale and empty, before six kingdoms curled protectively around a grey scar in the earth that no mapmaker would draw past. The old women called it the Void, and they did not look at it after dark, the way you don't look too long at a wound on someone else's body for fear of finding your own future in it. I grew up in its shadow the way another child grows up in the shadow of a mountain — an enormous, permanent fact of the landscape, present at every window, too large to really see. It never once felt to me like the Void was watching. That was the town's job, and Ashenmoor did it well.

I was small even for an elf, and elves are not a tall people to begin with, and I had ears the local boys liked to grab and yank until the tips went white and I cried without meaning to. That day there were three of them — the miller's son and the smith's son and a cousin of one or the other, I never did sort out which — and they had backed me behind the chapel where the priests couldn't see, into a strip of yard where the frost hadn't melted since the first cold snap and the mud underneath had gone hard and grey as old bone.

"Leaf-ears," the biggest one said. He always said it the same way, like it was a spell he was casting. "Cursed thing. My da says your kind brings the Void closer every year you keep breathing our air." He shoved me, and I went down hard onto the frozen ground, and something that had lived quietly in my chest my whole short life — coiled, patient, waiting for a reason — opened its eyes.

I felt it before I understood it. The air around me thickened, the way a room thickens before a storm breaks over it, pressing down on everything at once. It wasn't dramatic. It didn't need to be. It was just enough that the boy standing over me staggered, as though the ground itself had tilted a few degrees for him alone, and his friends' laughter caught wet in their throats and died there. I only knew, lying in the mud with my ears ringing and my whole body gone strange and heavy, that the world bent a little when I was frightened enough, and that it always made everything worse afterward. Marked. Cursed. A mage-child born to the wrong parents in the wrong town, and towns like mine did not forgive a girl for being marked, whatever she had or hadn't done to deserve it.

"She's doing it again," the smith's son hissed, already backing away. "Get the priest. Get—"

He never finished, because that was the exact moment the water rose out of the horse trough behind them, silent as a held breath, and slapped all three boys full in the face like a hand out of nowhere.

I looked up through my own wet lashes and found a boy standing on the low chapel wall with his palm still cupped toward the trough, watching three older boys splutter and swear below him with an expression of pure, uncomplicated delight. I knew his face, if not his name yet, from the chapel steps where I liked to sit and read when the weather allowed it. Kael — ten years old, human, with the kind of easy grin that made grown men nervous and made other children fall in behind him without ever quite deciding to. He looked like a boy who had been waiting his whole short life for a good enough reason to do exactly this.

"Funny," he said, to no one and everyone, "how brave three of you get around one girl half your size. Want to try it on somebody your own age? I'm right here." He hopped down off the wall without a flicker of concern, water still dripping off his sleeve where his own magic had run backward through him too — an affinity that answered him just as strangely as mine answered me, though nobody in Ashenmoor had ever thought to burn a boy for calling water the way they thought to burn a girl for bending the ground.

The miller's son spat mud and swore he'd tell his father. Kael told him to tell whoever he liked. The three of them went, dripping and furious and — I noticed, hiccupping through tears I hadn't let myself cry in front of them — a little afraid of him now too, though he'd never actually threatened a single one of them. He had simply decided, the way he seemed to decide most things, that the fight belonged to him, and somehow that alone had been enough to end it.

He crouched into the frozen mud without any apparent concern for his own knees and looked at me properly for the first time in my life. "You're the girl from the chapel steps," he said. "The one who reads." I nodded, because I didn't trust my voice to do anything but shake. "You're shaking," he said — not unkindly, just as a fact — and shrugged out of his own coat and settled it around my shoulders like he'd done it a hundred times before, though we had never once spoken until that morning. "It's the Void, you know," he added, nodding past the tree line toward the grey nothing none of us were supposed to look at too long. "Makes people stupid and mean, living this close to it for so many years. My mother says it's like a bruise on the whole world, and everybody in Ashenmoor's been standing on the same bruise long enough to forget it hurts."

"They say I'll make it worse," I managed. "That mages like me are the reason it keeps spreading."

"They say a lot of things." He helped me up, unhurried, as if there were no possible version of that morning in which he had somewhere better to be. Past the square behind him stood the black iron frame the town kept for burnings, swept and empty and waiting the way it always was — a fixture as ordinary to Ashenmoor as the well, there to remind us all exactly what happened to the truly cursed, the ones the Church never claimed as its own. I had watched a woman die on that frame two winters before, and I had not slept right for the better part of a month afterward. Mages, at least — real ones, marked and catalogued and collared under the Church's strange, cold protection — did not end there. It was almost a comfort, in its way, knowing precisely which terrible fate was not yet mine to fear.

Kael didn't look at the frame at all. He was watching my face instead, like it mattered more. "Come on," he said. "I know where the miller keeps his good apples, and after that you can show me what a leaf-ear does when nobody's trying to drown her for it."

I laughed — a small, disbelieving, painful sound, like something long unused finding its hinges again after years of rust — and for the first time in longer than I could remember, the air around me didn't feel like it was waiting for a reason to fall.

— end of Chapter One —

Chapter Two

Duskhaven Academy

as remembered by Lyra

The Warden came to Ashenmoor once a year, the same way the tax collector came, or the traveling priest, except the whole town feared him for a different reason entirely. He rode in on a black gelding branded with the Church's sigil, lined the parish children in rows outside the chapel, and passed his open palm an inch above each small head, watching to see whose hair lifted, whose breath fogged in warm air, whose shadow bent the wrong way against the frost. I was eleven the year his hand stopped over mine and did not move on.

"Lunar," he said, mostly to himself, writing it into a ledger bound in grey leather. "Rare, that. Girl'll need proper handling or she'll crack something important one day — herself, most likely." He looked at me the way the millers looked at good grain, weighing and pleased, and I understood, even at eleven, that I had just become someone's inventory. Then his palm passed over Kael's head and the candle two feet away guttered as if a window had opened somewhere it shouldn't have. The Warden's eyebrows rose. "Water, and strong. Both of you, then. Duskhaven will want you both."

Our priest tried to argue that the boy wasn't marked the way the girl was, that surely one mage-child a year was tithe enough for a town our size. The Warden looked at him with the flat patience of a man who had had this same argument in forty other villages and won every time. "The Church protects what serves it," he said. "An untrained water-mage is a drowned barn waiting to happen. Would you rather explain that to the Bishop, or would you rather I take him somewhere he can be useful instead of dangerous?" Nobody in Ashenmoor argued with the Church twice.

My grandmother didn't cry while she packed my things, though her hands shook the whole time she folded my one good dress, and she pressed her own shawl into my arms at the door as if wool could say what she couldn't. "Warm enough for the coast," was all she told me, and I understood, the way children understand things they aren't old enough to name yet, that this was the only goodbye she had left in her to give. Three weeks later Kael and I rode out of town in the back of a covered cart, that shawl still smelling of woodsmoke around my shoulders, and I did not look back at the black iron frame in the square as it shrank behind us. Some things are easier to leave if you don't watch them get smaller.

Duskhaven Academy sat on a cliff above a grey, restless sea, four days' ride from anywhere I had ever been — a fortress of black stone the Church had built less to keep enemies out than to keep three hundred unpredictable children contained in one place at once. Magistra Voss met us at the gate: a stern, sharp-boned woman in her thirties with a permanent ink stain on her right hand and, as far as I could tell that whole first year, no patience whatsoever for crying, of which the new intake did a great deal that week. "You are not prisoners," she told us, pacing in front of the assembled first-years like she was inspecting livestock she hadn't yet decided to buy. "You are assets the Church has chosen not to burn. I would recommend gratitude, or at minimum, competence. Missions begin at fifteen. Until then, you will learn to control what you are, so that when you are finally useful, you do not kill your supervisor by accident." A boy near the back laughed, thinking it a joke. Magistra Voss did not smile, and neither, after a moment, did he.

The dormitories were cold stone and thin blankets, and the rest of us sorted ourselves quickly into two kinds: the ones who'd grown up wealthy enough to resent being sent here, and the ones who, like me, found that three meals a day and a locked door against the world outside felt like more mercy than we'd expected to get from anyone. Kael adapted to Duskhaven the way he adapted to everything — cheerfully, immediately, charming the kitchen staff into extra bread within a week and finding his first fistfight within two, over nothing more than a boy who'd called me a leech on the Church's charity within my hearing. He came back to the dormitory with a split lip and no regrets at all. "Ashenmoor rules don't stop just because we left Ashenmoor," he told me, dabbing at his mouth with the back of his sleeve. "Somebody has to keep doing them."

At night, once the other first-years were asleep, I practiced alone in the courtyard under the moon Magistra Voss had told us all to respect and fear in equal measure, feeling the ground answer me a little more each time, feeling myself grow strange and strong and slightly less afraid of my own hands. Kael usually came to watch, sitting on the cold steps with his knees pulled up, not saying much — just there, the same way he'd been there in the mud behind the chapel, proof, every single night, that being marked didn't have to mean being alone with it.

— end of Chapter Two —

Chapter Three

Lessons of Blood and Silver

as remembered by Lyra

By sixteen, I could drop a grown man to his knees from across a courtyard without touching him, and I'd learned, more slowly and at greater cost, when not to. Duskhaven taught control first and compassion second, if it taught compassion at all — Magistra Voss's favorite lesson was that a mage who hesitated was a mage who got her whole party killed, and she illustrated the point with a scar on her own forearm often enough that nobody doubted her.

Our first real mission, the summer we turned sixteen, was meant to be simple: escort a merchant caravan past Greywater, where Void-sickness sometimes crept into the livestock and turned them wrong in the night. It was not simple. We arrived to find the town's square already occupied — a woman lashed to a post, kindling stacked to her knees, a crowd of ordinary, unremarkable townsfolk watching with the flat, hungry patience of people who had been told this was justice and had decided to believe it. "Void-touched," Magistra Voss said quietly, reading the charge nailed above the woman's head, and didn't let either of us look away. "Not a mage. No collar, no ledger entry, no Church claim on her. That is the only reason she is not on our ledger and safe on the coach. Remember the difference. It is the only thing standing between you and that post."

I didn't sleep that night, and Kael found me on the roof of the coaching inn, knees against my chest, the way he always did. He didn't offer comfort so much as company, which by then I'd learned was the better gift. "I keep thinking," I said, "that it's luck. All of it. That I could've been born three villages that way and been her instead of me." "You weren't," Kael said. "You were born the exact amount of trouble you are, and I like you exactly that much trouble." I laughed despite myself, and when I looked at him in the dark I noticed, not for the first time but for the first time on purpose, the particular way he was looking back at me — like the mission and the fire and the whole cruel machinery of the Church had receded somewhere far away and there was only the two of us on a roof.

Neither of us said anything more that night. Neither of us needed to. It became, over the following year, a fact as ordinary and load-bearing as the ground: Kael and I, the water-mage and the moon-marked girl, inseparable in the dormitory halls in the way that made the younger students roll their eyes and the older ones grow quietly protective, as if the two of us being happy was a fragile, communal thing worth guarding.

Our missions grew harder as our control grew steadier — a haunting at Millbrook that turned out to be smugglers exploiting superstition, a Void-tainted wolf pack outside Corrin that took three days and cost another team's mage a hand, a riot in Ashford that the Church's presence made worse before it made better, though no one above Magistra Voss's rank seemed interested in that particular lesson. I learned to read a battlefield the way I'd once learned to read a room full of bullies: where the weight was, where it would move, where it could be made to fall. Kael learned to be the water in every fight that needed drowning and the water in every wound that needed cleaning, and between the two of us, our small three-person unit earned a reputation Magistra Voss pretended not to be proud of.

It was in our last spring at Duskhaven, the week before graduation missions began in earnest, that Kael finally said it plainly, on the same roof, under a moon just short of full. "I love you," he said, like it was the least remarkable thing in the world, like he'd simply run out of reasons to keep it to himself. "I have for years. I just wanted you strong enough first that you'd believe I meant it and not think you needed saving to earn it." I didn't cry, though I came close. "I already knew," I told him. "I just liked hearing you be brave enough to say it out loud." We had, neither of us then knew, one mission left before the world decided that wasn't going to be allowed to last.

— end of Chapter Three —

Interlude

What the Water Boy Carried

as remembered by Kael

I never told Lyra this, because there never seemed to be a right moment and then, all at once, there wasn't any moment left at all — but I had decided I loved her three years before I said so out loud on that roof, and I spent every one of those three years far more afraid of being wrong about it than I ever was of anything Duskhaven asked us to fight.

Water doesn't choose where it goes. That's the first thing they teach a water-mage, right after they teach you not to drown your own classmates by accident: water finds the lowest point and fills whatever shape it's given, patient and shapeless, impossible to hold onto by force. I used to think that made me the opposite of Lyra, whose gravity pulled everything toward her whether it wanted to come or not, heavy and inevitable, a fixed point in a room. It took me years to understand I had it backward. I wasn't shapeless. I had simply already chosen my shape, the day I was ten years old and decided the fight behind that chapel belonged to me. I just hadn't told anyone, myself included, exactly what I'd chosen it for.

My mother used to say I was born already knowing how to want things, which she meant as a complaint and I always took as a compliment. She stayed behind in Ashenmoor when the Warden took me — too old to be reassigned to a life near the coast, she said, and too stubborn besides — and I wrote to her every season whether or not I had anything worth telling. I never told her about Lyra directly. I think she knew anyway. Mothers generally do.

I used to lie awake in the dormitory some nights, after the lamps went out, and build the whole rest of my life like a house with no walls yet, just a shape I liked the outline of: the two of us somewhere the sea wasn't grey, once our years of Church service finally ran out and nobody could tell either of us where to point our own magic anymore. I never said this plan out loud. It felt like the kind of thing that could only survive if you didn't test it against the actual world too early.

The morning we rode out for Corsen, I packed my kit the way I always did — quickly, cheerfully, no ceremony — and told myself the small cold feeling sitting low in my chest was nothing, the same nerves I got before every mission, nothing a decent breakfast wouldn't fix. I have never believed in premonitions. I still don't, entirely, even knowing how that day ended. But I remember deciding, without quite deciding out loud, that I would walk point going up into those hills instead of letting one of the seniors take it, and I remember not telling Lyra why, because saying it would have meant admitting I was afraid of something I couldn't name, and I had spent my whole life being the one who wasn't afraid of anything. It was easier to just do it than to explain it.

She caught my sleeve at the stable door anyway, the way she always did before a mission, quick and unshowy, more habit than gesture by then. "Don't be a hero," she told me — the same thing she told me before every mission, in the same dry tone, like it was a joke between us by then instead of the thing she actually meant.

"Never," I told her, which was, I want it noted, a complete and total lie. And we rode out toward Corsen together, the whole unwritten rest of my life folded up small and certain in my chest, waiting for a future I fully intended to get to.

— end of interlude —

Chapter Four

What the Water Boy Left

as remembered by Lyra

The mission was called simple by everyone who assigned it and by no one who survived it. A Void-tear had opened in the hills above a hamlet called Corsen — smaller than the one that had swallowed the land past Ashenmoor, but tears didn't stay small, and the Church wanted it sealed before it did what tears always eventually did. Magistra Voss led our party of six: herself, Kael, me, and three graduating seniors I'd trained beside for two years. It should have been, by every measure the academy had taught us, well within our strength.

What came out of the tear wasn't on any ledger the Church kept. It moved like smoke deciding to be an animal, and it screamed in a register that made the horses bolt and two of the seniors freeze solid with terror before it had even touched either of them. I dropped it to its knees with everything I had, the whole weight of the moon behind my hands, and it should have been enough — it would have been enough, against anything the ledgers had prepared us for. It wasn't enough. It rose again, wrong-jointed and furious, and turned for me the instant my strength guttered low from the effort of the first blow.

Kael put himself between us without a word of warning, the same way he'd put himself between me and three boys behind a chapel seven years before, except this time the thing he was fighting didn't flinch at a face full of water. It went through his water-shield the way the tear went through everything the world understood, and it went through him a half-second later, and by the time my own magic came roaring back with a scream I don't remember deciding to make, there was nothing left in front of me to save.

I held what was left of him while Magistra Voss and the last standing senior finally closed the tear behind us, and I didn't stop screaming for a long time — not sobbing, not weeping quietly the way grief is supposed to look in the stories, but screaming, raw and endless, the sound tearing out of my chest until my voice gave out and I kept making the shape of it anyway, silent, my whole body shaking with a sound that had nowhere left to go. Magistra Voss didn't tell me to stop. Nobody who had earned the right to speak that night told me to stop.

We buried him three days later on the cliffside above Duskhaven, facing the sea he'd never much cared about but which was, I thought, at least honest about being enormous and indifferent, unlike the Church that stood over the grave and called his death a sacrifice in service of the light, as if light had been anywhere on that hillside when he died. I didn't cry at the funeral. I'd done all my crying already, in the dark, with his weight in my arms, and what came after the crying, I discovered, was something colder and stiller and far more useful — a calm that settled over me the way frost settles over a field, not an absence of feeling but a decision about where to put it. I thanked the priest for his words. I shook Magistra Voss's hand. I asked, in a voice that surprised everyone including myself with how steady it was, when my next mission would be.

Chapter Five

The Long Quiet

as remembered by Lyra

I finished my last year at Duskhaven the way I did everything after Corsen: competently, quietly, without complaint and without warmth. My marks were the best in my year. My missions came back clean. Magistra Voss, who had buried enough students to know the difference between a girl healing and a girl simply going very still, watched me the way you watch a bridge after a flood — not doubting it will hold, exactly, just aware that something underneath it has changed.

On the morning of my graduation, Voss offered me a permanent commission — a seat on one of the Church's standing mission companies, good pay, a title, the kind of security most students would have wept for. I turned it down so evenly that Voss set her pen aside and actually looked at me. "You're angry at the ledger," she said. It wasn't a question. "You think if he hadn't been Church property, someone might have called that mission off before it killed him." "I think," I said, "that I'd rather choose which fights I take from now on, and answer for the choosing myself." Voss studied me for a long moment, then nodded once, the closest thing to approval I'd ever gotten out of her. "Independent contract-mages die just as easily," she warned. "Just with nobody writing it down." "Then I'll be careful," I said, "and I'll write it down myself."

I left Duskhaven on a grey morning with a mage's traveling license, a chest of gear I'd earned rather than been issued, and no one to see me off but Voss, standing at the gate with her arms crossed against the cold. "You were the best thing to come out of that boy's death," Voss said, gruffly, which was as near to a eulogy as either of us would ever give Kael out loud. "Don't waste it feeling guilty for surviving him." I didn't answer that. I simply nodded, shouldered my pack, and walked down the cliff road toward the rest of my life, and if I cried once I was out of sight of the fortress, I never told anyone, and the moon, rising early and pale over the grey sea, didn't ask me to explain myself.

The years that followed blurred the way years do when a person is working hard at not thinking too closely about any single one of them. I took contracts other mages wouldn't — Void-fringe work, mostly, the kind that paid well because it killed people who hesitated, and I'd stopped hesitating the day Kael stopped breathing. I built a name for myself under a name that wasn't quite my own — the Grey Moon, some frightened farmer had first called me, and it stuck the way nicknames stick to people who never bother to correct them. I was good at the work. I was, if I was honest with the only person who ever asked me to be — myself, at three in the morning, staring at ceilings — very lonely in exactly the specific shape Kael had left behind.

Chapter Six

The Auction at Kirrow

as remembered by Lyra

Kirrow was a trade city built on a harbor and a shrug, the kind of place where six kingdoms' worth of law arrived at the docks and quietly agreed to stop applying. I'd come for a contract — a merchant house wanted a Void-tainted warehouse cleared before the harvest ships arrived — and I had four days to wait before the paperwork cleared, which was how I came to be walking through the market square on auction day at all.

I had seen slave markets before, from a professional distance, the way a person learns to see a great many ugly things and keep walking because there is always a contract waiting and a room to pay for. I had never quite made myself watch one. I made myself watch this one, and wished immediately that I hadn't.

The girl on the block was small even measured against my own memory of being small — elf-blooded, unmistakably, ears notched with old scars where someone had pierced them and torn the rings free more than once, and she stood on that platform with the particular flat stillness of a child who had learned that flinching only made things worse. The auctioneer was praising her hardiness, her youth, the years of service still in her, in the same tone a man might use to sell a good mule, and the crowd was murmuring numbers like it was any other Tuesday, and something in me — old, coiled, familiar — opened its eyes for the first time in years.

I don't remember deciding to move. I remember being at the front of the crowd, the weight of the whole square pressing down at my call the way it hadn't since Corsen, the auctioneer's ledger sliding off the podium on its own, and my own voice, flat and very cold, naming a sum that ended the bidding before it started. I remember the trader's face when I paid it — the particular fear of a man realizing, too late, exactly what he'd been selling in front of. I remember crouching down afterward, level with the girl's eyes, and saying the only thing that felt true. "You don't have to flinch anymore. Not around me. I need you to believe that eventually, even if it isn't today."

The girl didn't answer. She hadn't spoken, I'd learn later, in longer than the trader had bothered to track. But she didn't flinch either, when I reached out and, very slowly, cut the rope from her wrists with a knife instead of a spell, because a spell would have been faster and a knife let her watch it happen and know, with her own eyes, exactly when it ended. "My name's Lyra," I said. "What's yours?" It took three streets and one shared meal of bread and cheese before she finally answered, in a voice rusted almost to nothing from disuse. "Wren," she said. "It used to be Wren."

"It still is," I told her, "if you want it to be. That's yours now. Nobody gets to take it again." Wren looked at me for a long moment with eyes far older than the rest of her, then did something I hadn't expected and would remember for the rest of my life: she reached out, small and uncertain, and took my hand.

— end of Chapter Six —

Chapter Seven

Two Ghosts, One House

as remembered by Lyra

I rented three rooms above a cooper's shop in a quiet river town well clear of Kirrow — the first fixed address I'd kept since Duskhaven — and told myself it was only sensible. A growing child needed somewhere that didn't move. I didn't examine too closely why it felt like the first thing I'd built instead of merely survived, not since the cliffside above Duskhaven.

Wren didn't speak much, not at first, and I'd learned enough from my own long silence after Corsen not to push. Instead I put a slate and a stick of chalk on the table every morning and simply started, without ceremony, the way you teach a skill rather than announce a gift: letters first, then words, then the particular satisfaction of a sentence she'd made entirely by herself. She took to it with a hunger that told me, more clearly than any story she never told, exactly how much had been kept from her on purpose. Within a season she was reading contracts over my shoulder and correcting my arithmetic on market days, smug about it in a way that made me laugh out loud for the first time in longer than I wanted to admit.

The rest of the lessons were more practical. I taught her which roadside plants stopped bleeding and which ones started it, how to splint a wrist and pack a wound and know the difference between a fever that needed watching and one that needed running for a healer. I taught her the careful, patient chemistry of a proper potion kit — which herbs wanted crushing and which wanted whole, why you never rushed a simmer, how to tell a healing draught gone right from one gone dangerously wrong by smell alone before it ever touched a tongue. And in the evenings, in the yard behind the cooper's shop, I taught her to fight — not to hurt, not yet, just to move, to block, to fall without breaking. We sparred with wooden practice blades until we were both laughing too hard to keep proper form, her small victories over my deliberately slow guard met with a fierce, delighted pride that had nothing left in it of the girl on the auction block.

I didn't tell her about Kael for a long time, and when I finally did, one night by the fire when she asked, very carefully, why I sometimes went somewhere else behind my own eyes, I told her the whole of it plainly, because I'd decided early that she'd been lied to enough for one childhood and didn't need a second adult who edited the truth for her comfort. She listened without interrupting, and when I finished, she didn't offer comfort so much as company, in a small, solemn voice that broke my heart and mended some other part of it at exactly the same moment. "I'm sorry he died," she said. "I'm glad you didn't stay sad enough to leave me on that platform, though. Is that a bad thing to be glad about?" "No," I told her, throat tight. "That's the least bad thing either of us has felt in years."

— end of Chapter Seven —

Chapter Eight

The Contract at Hollow Bridge

as remembered by Lyra

The job at Hollow Bridge was meant to be mine alone — a toll-road plagued by bandits using a collapsed span as a chokepoint, simple work for a mage of my reputation — but Wren, twelve now and stubborn in the particular way of a child who's decided she's done being left in safe rooms, had packed her own kit the night before without asking and presented herself at the stable door already dressed for travel. "I can hold the horses. I can watch the road. I can bandage you if it goes wrong," she said, in the flat, unarguable tone she'd clearly practiced. "You taught me all of it. Let me use it."

I could have said no. I thought about Corsen, about the particular arithmetic of who gets hurt when someone insists on being useful past their training, and I very nearly did say no. But I looked at her face — steady, sure, nothing at all like the flinching child on the auction block — and understood that saying no now would teach her exactly the wrong lesson about what all that training had been for. "You do exactly what I tell you, exactly when I tell you," I said instead. "The second I say run, you run, no arguing, no looking back to see if I'm following. Agreed?" "Agreed," she said, so fast it was clear she'd have agreed to anything.

The bandits at Hollow Bridge were better organized than the contract had suggested — eleven of them dug into the collapsed span with a captured toll-keeper as leverage — and the fight that followed was ugly and close and went on twice as long as I'd planned for. It was Wren, crouched exactly where I'd told her to stay, who spotted the second group circling from the tree line and screamed the warning half a second before they'd have had me flanked — not a spell, not a blade, just a clear, carrying voice that gave me the half-second I needed to turn the fight instead of losing it. Afterward, with the toll-keeper freed and the bandits scattered or bound, I found her exactly where I'd left her, hands shaking, blood that wasn't hers on her sleeve from where she'd already started packing a wound on a fallen guard without being asked.

"You did well," I told her, meaning it completely, crouching to check the bandage myself and finding nothing to correct. Her chin came up, fierce and proud and trying very hard not to show how frightened she'd been a moment before. "I told you I could," she said. I almost laughed, except my chest was too tight with something that felt uncomfortably like the particular pride of a parent — a word I'd never once let myself use out loud for what this had become between us. "You did," I agreed instead, and let that be enough for both of us, all the way back down the mountain road toward home.

— end of Chapter Eight —

Interlude

What the Wren Kept

as remembered by Wren

I don't remember most of the auction block. I've tried, some nights, turning it over like a stone to see what's underneath, and mostly what's underneath is nothing — a fog I'd learned to make for myself long before Lyra ever cut the rope from my wrists, the same trick a body does to survive a winter it doesn't expect to walk out of. What I remember instead is smaller and stranger: the exact weight of her hand when she finally took mine, warm in a way I hadn't let myself notice hands could be.

Trust, for me, wasn't a door that opened all at once. It was a hundred small tests I never told her I was running, and she passed every one without ever knowing there was an exam. She never raised her voice at me, not once, not even the week I broke her good inkwell testing whether she would. She never made me eat before her, or after her, or in any particular order at all — she just put two plates on the table, like that had always been the shape of things. It took me the better part of a year to stop flinching at raised hands that weren't even raised at me. She never once told me to hurry up and heal.

She taught me to read before she taught me to fight, and I understood even then that the order mattered — that she wanted me to have words of my own before she gave me anything that could be mistaken for a weapon. When the fighting lessons finally came, they came slow and deliberately unthreatening, wooden blades and a guard she kept loose enough for me to beat on purpose, and I understood that too, eventually: she was teaching me that my own strength could belong to me instead of to whoever decided next to take it. Nobody had ever built anything for me on purpose before. I still don't quite have the word for what that felt like, except to say it felt like being unbroken instead of merely healed.

The night she finally told me about Kael, I already half knew, the way you know a house has an empty room even before anyone shows it to you — she went somewhere else behind her eyes sometimes, somewhere I couldn't follow, and I'd learned the shape of her leaving long before I learned its cause. I asked because I wanted her to know I could hold something heavy for her, the way she'd been holding things heavy for me since the day she paid for me like I was worth more than the number some trader named. I meant what I told her that night, about being glad. I've never taken it back.

Hollow Bridge was the first time she let me matter somewhere that could actually kill me, and I understood, packing my kit the night before without asking a permission I knew she wouldn't give if I asked it properly, that I was choosing it the same way she'd once chosen a knife over a faster spell — slower, on purpose, so I'd have to feel myself doing it and know I meant it. I was frightened in a way I hadn't let myself be since the auction block, and I discovered, crouched in the trees with my heart going like a hammer, that the fear was mine to carry now rather than something being done to me, and that the difference between those two things was the whole of what she'd spent three years teaching me.

She calls it pride, the tightness in her chest when she looks at me like that. I think it's something else too, something neither of us has the nerve to name yet out loud, the way she never named what Kael was to her until it was almost too late to say it at all. I've decided I won't make that mistake twice on her behalf. So here it is, plainly, the way she taught me plain things matter most: she is the reason Wren is a name I get to keep instead of one that was simply left over. Whatever this is between us, I intend to go on calling it exactly what it's starting to feel like. Family. She can argue with the word later, if she wants to. I learned patience from someone who let a knife take longer than a spell, just so I'd know, with my own eyes, exactly when the cutting was finished.

— end of interlude —

Chapter Nine

Salt and Iron

as remembered by Lyra

By the time the Continental Guild sent riders to find me instead of waiting for me to find them, seven years had passed since Kirrow, and Wren had grown taller than the girl who'd once fit inside my traveling cloak with room to spare. I'd stopped bothering to correct the name people used for me by then. The Grey Moon had outgrown Lyra of Ashenmoor in every tavern between the coast and the eastern hills, and I'd learned there was no use arguing with a legend that paid better than the truth.

The Guild kept its own ledger, and it had nothing to do with the Church's — no collar, no tithe, no priest weighing whether you were a blessing or a curse before he'd let you work. It measured one thing only: whether you came back from the jobs nobody else would take, and how many people were still breathing because you had. Ranks ran from F to S on a board in every guildhall from the coast to the eastern hills, and for most of my working life I'd watched other names climb it while mine sat quietly among the B's, useful and unremarkable. That changed the season I went into the Hollow Marsh alone, after three better-funded parties had gone in ahead of me and not come out.

I won't pretend I remember all of it clearly. I remember mud to my knees and a sky the wrong color for that hour of the afternoon. I remember the tear itself, wider than any I'd faced since Corsen, breathing out something that wasn't smoke and wasn't fog and dropped the marsh birds dead out of the air if they flew too close to it. I remember closing it the way you close a wound you already know is going to scar badly no matter how careful your hands are, and I remember waking two days later in a healer's tent in Fenmarch with my hair gone half white at the temple — a souvenir I still carry — and a Guild courier already waiting outside to write down everything I could tell him.

They gave me an iron ring instead of a medal, plain and unengraved, the kind a smith could make in an afternoon rather than a jeweler in a month — the Guild's idea of honesty, I think, about what the work actually was. "A-Rank," the Guildmaster told me, not smiling, sliding it across the table like it might still bite someone. "There are three others living who wear one of these. There's exactly one name above all of ours, in a rank we stopped pretending we understood how to award — S, on a plaque so old the paint's gone soft, and not one Guildmaster in three generations has met the person who earned it. You aren't that. Nobody expects you to be. But you're the closest thing this Guild has produced in a decade, and I'd rather you heard it from me than read it exaggerated off a wanted-work board in Kirrow."

Wren whooped loud enough to startle a whole stable of guild horses when I told her, and made me wear the ring to supper that night, though it caught on everything and I nearly lost a finger to a soup ladle proving it worked. She had her own rank by then, a hard-earned C she was prouder of than I had ever been of anything at her age, and she toasted me with cider like the whole tavern belonged to us for the evening, which, for one evening, it very nearly did. I let myself enjoy it. I want that on the record, whatever comes after — I let myself enjoy it completely, without once looking over my shoulder to see who in that crowded, cheering room might be counting exactly how easy I'd be to find, later, once somebody decided that being the best-known mage in six kingdoms was a very different thing from being a safe one.

— end of Chapter Nine —

Chapter Ten

The Church's Long Shadow

as remembered by Lyra

The trouble had a name eight months before it ever had a face: the Writ of the Unbound, nailed to chapel doors in six kingdoms before midsummer, declaring that any mage working outside a parish collar and a Church ledger entry was to be considered a danger to the realm, regardless of guild rank, contract history, or public reputation. I read my own name in the pamphlet that came with it before I'd finished my second cup of tea in a coaching inn outside Fenmarch — the Grey Moon, cited by name as exactly the kind of uncontrolled power the Writ existed to correct.

Wren read it over my shoulder twice before she said anything, which for her was practically an essay of alarm. "They can't just—" she started. "They can," I said, "and they have, on paper. Paper hasn't caught me yet." I meant it lightly. I did not, if I'm honest with the memory rather than the version of myself I wanted to be that morning, feel it lightly at all.

The High Warden behind it, a man named Emeric Coldwell who'd risen through the Consistory faster than anyone that cautious usually rises, gave a sermon in Ashford market square not three weeks later that I stood at the back of, hood up, close enough to hear every word. He never said my name once. He didn't need to. He spoke instead of a woman with a silver mark and a Guild ring who let common folk mistake gratitude for safety, who had, in his words, "convinced an entire generation that a leash is the same thing as a friend simply because it does not currently pull." The crowd murmured agreement in places I recognized — a farmer whose barn I'd once cleared of a Void-taint infestation, nodding along like he'd never met me at all.

I didn't tell Wren I'd been there until she noticed the set of my jaw hadn't softened by supper. "You should be angry," she said, when I finally told her. "I am angry," I said, which was true, though underneath the anger, quieter and far less useful, was something closer to grief — for a version of the world where being good at protecting people was still allowed to be enough, on its own, without a collar to make it official. Wren wanted to march back and argue with a market square. I wanted, more than I let her see, to be wrong about how this particular story usually ended for mages who let themselves become famous instead of merely feared.

I've turned that sermon over more times since than I ever admitted to her, hunting for the exact moment I should have understood that a market square full of people I'd personally kept alive could still be talked into believing I was the danger. I never found it. I don't think there was one single moment. I think it's simply what fear does, given enough time and a patient enough voice telling it exactly where to point.

— end of Chapter Ten —

Chapter Eleven

A City That Remembers

as remembered by Lyra

I'd avoided Corsen for eleven years, the way you avoid a tooth you know is going to hurt if you press it, and I might have gone on avoiding it indefinitely if the Guild posting hadn't named the exact valley — a Void-taint bloom spreading from the same scarred ground where Kael died, patient and slow and entirely uninterested in how long I'd spent staying away from it.

The town had grown since I'd last seen it, enough that I didn't recognize the inn where we took rooms, but the valley itself hadn't changed at all — the same scorched treeline, the same wrongness in the grass that never quite grew back right. An old woman at the market recognized me before I'd said a word, grabbed my sleeve with both hands, and thanked me for something I only half remembered doing — a fever she'd survived, a decade ago, because a young mage passing through had known which draught to make. Not everyone in that square looked at me the same way. I noticed the ones who didn't. I always did, by then.

Closing the taint took three days and every trick I'd learned since the last time that ground had swallowed something precious out of me. I worked within sight of the exact rise where he'd fallen, close enough that I could've walked to it in under a minute, and didn't, the whole three days, though Wren caught me looking at it more than once and had the grace not to ask. On the last night, with the ground finally quiet under my hands for the first time in eleven years, I let myself walk up there after everyone else was asleep and sat where I thought the spot probably was, close enough for memory's sake if not for certainty's, and told him, out loud, foolishly, everything he'd missed. Wren. The Guild ring. All of it. The valley didn't answer. I hadn't expected it to. I said it anyway.

Not every conversation in Corsen went as gently as the old woman's. A younger man, maybe not yet born the year Kael died, spat near my boots outside the chapel and called me exactly the word Coldwell's sermon had been teaching people to use for months, and when the innkeeper — old enough to remember, old enough to still be grateful — told him sharply to hold his tongue, the young man didn't back down so much as go quiet in a way that frightened me more than shouting would have. I'd seen that particular quiet before, in every crowd that eventually decides shouting isn't necessary anymore.

We left Corsen the way I'd left it the first time — walking, no fanfare, the valley finally at rest behind us — except this time I understood, in a way sixteen-year-old me hadn't needed to yet, that some ground doesn't finish taking from you just because you've closed the wound in it. I thought I meant the valley. I've had a great deal of time since to wonder whether I actually meant the whole world.

— end of Chapter Eleven —

Chapter Twelve

The Informant

as remembered by Lyra

The man who found us in Duskhaven's lower market gave no name, which told me more about him than a name would have. He wore an acolyte's grey under a traveler's cloak too fine for either role, and he pressed something small and folded into Wren's hand instead of mine — the careful habit of a man who'd learned never to assume which of us was the one wearing the Guild ring. "Burn it after you've read it," he said, and was gone into the crowd before we'd finished turning to look for him.

It was a list. Six names, written in a hand too neat to belong to whoever had actually compiled it — a clerk's hand, copying someone else's order. Mine was the fourth. Coldwell had put a date beside each one, the way the Warden used to write a word in his grey ledger and move on to the next head of hair that lifted wrong in the cold. A fortnight, the note said, before the writs went to the city guard instead of staying quiet in some bishop's drawer. Whoever had sent it wanted that fortnight spent running, or spent somewhere a great deal braver than I felt standing in that market with the paper going damp in Wren's fist.

"Could be a trap," Wren said, which was also what I was thinking, and neither of us said the other obvious thing — that it could be true as well, and both of those were reasons to be afraid in exactly the same amount. I asked why anyone inside the Church would risk a man's own neck warning mages at all. Wren turned the paper over and showed me the reason I'd missed: a small mark pressed into the wax that had sealed it, a sigil I hadn't seen since Duskhaven Academy, half-remembered from a tutor who'd vanished partway through my second year without anyone quite explaining why.

I stood a long time with that memory before it resolved into anything useful. Not everyone the Church swallowed stayed swallowed, apparently. Some of them kept the shape of the thing they'd been trained to be and simply pointed it a different direction, quietly, for years, waiting for a use worth the risk. I didn't know yet whether that made the warning more trustworthy or less. I still don't, if I'm honest, even knowing now how the rest of it unfolded.

We didn't burn the list that night. I told myself it was evidence, the sensible mage's reasoning, the kind Wren could usually be talked into even when caution argued otherwise. The truer reason was smaller and far less sensible: six names felt like a weight I needed to carry a little longer before I let it go, the way you hold a bruise a moment after you've already confirmed it hurts. Four names besides my own. Four people who didn't yet know a fortnight was all the ordinary life they had left, and one sigil pressed into wax that meant somebody, somewhere inside the thing that had spent my whole life deciding what I was, had chosen — for reasons I couldn't yet guess at — not to let that happen without a warning first.

— end of Chapter Twelve —
